# Rehearsing We: choosing the first experiment

## Piece brief

The following description was supplied by the creator of the performance:

> What if the next superintelligence is a room full of people who just met? Join this participatory performance to become part of a temporary collective mind.
>
> Everyone speaks at once into their phones, the room’s thoughts instantly turn into a map of connections and contradictions. Find your intellectual nemesis, become unlikely collaborators, then get your hands on the rules and change how the whole thing works.
>
> The performance experiments with technologies for collective power. The ambition is outrageous: to become capable of organising our lives together at a scale and complexity usually reserved for corporations and states.
>
> Let’s rehearse what we’ve been told we could never pull off together.

Collective Microphone currently rehearses the collective-writing part of this
performance. Mapping connections, pairing participants and changing the room's
rules are part of the wider artistic brief, not implemented Stage 1 capabilities.
The tool should also work with other subjects: keep topics in scores, invitations
and configuration rather than in the composition engine.

## Status and central recommendation

This is a **proposal for discussion and rehearsal**, not an agreed performance
script or a replacement for the existing example score. The criteria and prompts
below are design judgements informed by the piece's purpose and the sources at
the end. They are not a validated recipe for collective intelligence.

The broad theme is collective power. The first experiment needs a more concrete
shared question:

> What would it take for us to have a real say over the things we depend on?

This lets ordinary experiences open into questions about who controls work,
housing, care, infrastructure, information and public decisions. It also makes
space for people who want accountable institutions, people who want to organise
their own alternatives, and people who distrust collective processes.

Feeling collective, reaching an understanding and acquiring power are different
outcomes. The experiment should make their relationship discussable. A fluent
collective passage cannot establish that participants agree, have authorised a
decision, or possess the means to enact it.

## Criteria for a productive topic

| Criterion                            | Practical test                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Easy entry                           | Can someone begin with an ordinary example without understanding political theory or having organising experience?                     |
| Different knowledge and stakes       | Can people contribute genuinely different experiences, constraints and proposals, including ambivalence about joining a group?         |
| A shared focus                       | Can participants recognise what their contributions have to do with one another, even when their situations differ?                    |
| Consequential disagreement           | Could two serious responses conflict over a decision, cost or responsibility, rather than only use different words for agreement?      |
| Material and institutional traction  | Can the inquiry reach who controls a resource or decision, what that control depends on, and what people could change together?        |
| Unequal burdens are visible          | Can someone ask who has time, who does the work, who bears risk, who benefits and who remains outside the group?                       |
| Capacity to develop                  | Can a contribution change a proposal or expose a new problem, giving the next response something specific to answer?                   |
| Freedom to withhold and dissent      | Can someone offer an observed or hypothetical example, decline personal disclosure, reject the framing, or refuse the proposed action? |
| A connection to the room's own rules | Can the topic eventually help participants examine who speaks for them and alter how a collective decision or representation is made?  |

These are selection criteria, not a numerical score. A long list of different
opinions is insufficient if nothing anyone says changes the inquiry. Likewise,
disagreement is not useful merely because it produces heat. Look for objections
that force a proposal to account for a previously ignored person or constraint.

## Candidate openings

| Invitation                                                                                                         | Strength                                                                              | Limitation                                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Think of something you depend on but have little say over. Tell us about one decision you wish you could change." | Everyday entry with a clear route towards power and collective agency.                | Starts from a deficit; follow with capacities and successful experience, rather than collecting grievances indefinitely.              |
| "Tell us about a time you tried to change something with other people. What helped, or got in the way?"            | Concrete stories reveal organising knowledge, failure and unexpected success.         | Can privilege experienced organisers or imply that everyone has already tried. Permit witnessed examples and reasons for not joining. |
| "What would you need before you could trust a group to act in your name?"                                          | Directly connects the collective voice to representation, consent and accountability. | Can remain about interpersonal trust unless followed by decisions, resources and enforceable responsibilities.                        |

Use the first invitation for a mixed room of strangers. Offer the second as a
later invitation to bring practical knowledge into the room. The third becomes
especially useful after participants have encountered a generated "we".

Allow a small, everyday, witnessed or imagined example. Nobody has to identify
an employer, disclose a personal injury, or demonstrate political commitment.
Give examples only when people need them; a list of suggested issues can determine
what the room talks about before it has begun.

## A proposed progression

### 1. Experience: what do we want a say over?

Start with the recommended opening. After a first exchange, invite the room to
notice a recurring mechanism or choose a situation to explore more closely.
Avoid treating a missed bus, workplace retaliation and loss of housing as
interchangeable harms merely because each involves limited control.

### 2. Capacity: what would let us act together?

> What would it take for people affected by that decision to change it together?

Invite examples of something that has worked as well as missing conditions:
time, childcare, money, skills, organisation, infrastructure, public provision,
shared ownership or the ability to make a credible commitment. Ask how a named
condition changes what people can do. "More solidarity" needs a concrete meaning.

### 3. Counterpower: why could we not simply be ignored?

> What could we do together that would make the people controlling this decision have to respond?

Probe the mechanism rather than prescribing a tactic. Who holds the decision?
What do they depend on? What can the affected people withhold, redirect, secure,
build or govern? Where would legal authority, organised labour, collective
provision or an institution help? Who could afford the costs, and what would make
participation possible for those who could not?

Recognise limits. Finding an opponent does not itself establish leverage, and
the room cannot infer practical feasibility from the confidence of a proposal.

### 4. Governing our power: who can act for whom?

> If we gained that power, how would the people affected keep control of it?

Let concrete disagreements lead: fast decisions versus time to participate;
expert delegation versus recall; commitment versus the ability to refuse; broad
coalitions versus an excluded minority. Do not reduce every conflict to two
equally good preferences: some involve unequal losses or incompatible interests.

When the performance can actually change rules, test one relevant rule and ask
who gained influence and who lost it. The scale question then becomes concrete:
what can be decided here, what needs coordination elsewhere, and how would
delegates remain answerable? Large-scale coordination need not mean one room
reaching agreement about everything.

These are facilitator options, not four compulsory turns or new CLI features.
In responsive stages, the next invitation should follow what participants and
the collective have actually said. Stage 1 can only rehearse fixed material.

## Implications for the collective voice

Keep three distinct layers:

1. **Topic:** the shared field of inquiry.
2. **Participant invitation:** what someone is asked to speak about now.
3. **Composer instruction:** how admitted contributions become a passage.

Changing the topic alone will not fix a composer that keeps producing an abstract
third-person synopsis. For the next prompt experiment, try this draft instruction:

> Compose a short utterance the people in the room could answer. Work from the new contributions and use earlier passages only for continuity. Keep concrete actions, constraints and wording that matter. Make the new material change what is being said rather than recapping the whole history. Distinguish a desire, a proposal, a disagreement and a decision; never invent agreement, authorisation or a decision. If using "we", qualify differences where needed: "some of us", "others", or an unresolved question. Do not assign a participant a position they did not express. Return one or two speakable sentences.

This wording remains a draft. Test it separately from changing the source score
so that improvements or regressions can be attributed to a particular change.
First-person language alone does not establish a collective voice: it can still
turn a model's synthesis into an unauthorised claim of consensus.

"Intellectual nemesis" can mean a useful objection to a specific proposal. A
person can oppose one proposal and collaborate on another; topic design should
make these changing relationships possible rather than cast permanent opposing
characters. A later invitation is: "What did the collective just say that you
want to correct, resist or make more concrete?"

## Designing the next scripted score

Once the opening is chosen, write plainly spoken, explicitly fictional rehearsal
contributions. Use contrasting experiences and unequal room to act; avoid one
character per ideology or eight equally polished miniature manifestos.

Useful disagreements might include someone ready to organise but unable to lose
a shift, someone willing to delegate but demanding recall, someone who needs a
decision soon, and someone repeatedly excluded by the claim that everyone agreed.
Give each voice more than one concern and some capacity to surprise us. These
are examples of variation to explore, not demographic profiles or representative
claims about real participants.

Include at least one concrete success, failed attempt, practical proposal,
constraint and refusal. Allow agreement where it arises; do not manufacture
opposition to make the output appear lively. Give the score's opening question
and each change of invitation an explicit place in the rehearsal notes so that
inputs and outputs can be interpreted against the same purpose.

## How to judge the experiment

- Can a newcomer understand the invitation and start speaking without a political vocabulary lesson?
- Do the answers contain different situations and constraints, rather than the same desired outcome in different wording?
- Can we identify a decision or resource and explain how collective action might change its control?
- When an objection arrives, does the collective passage change, or merely add another abstract phrase?
- Can someone recognise a relationship to their contribution and also contest the synthesis?
- Are a connection, possible coalition, unresolved disagreement and actual decision kept distinct?
- Does the discussion expose the distribution of work and risk as well as the ambition to act?

For a small prompt pilot, ask people with differing amounts of organising
experience to respond to the invitation without further explanation. Listen for
requests for clarification, repeated abstract answers and examples that do not
connect. Treat that as evidence for revising the invitation. Synthetic text can
test composition behaviour but cannot establish how a human audience will react.

## Sources informing the design

The World Café recommends questions connected to participants' lives and allows
a progressively deeper inquiry over several rounds. That supports the proposed
move from everyday experience towards institutional questions; it does not prove
that this particular sequence will work for the performance.
[World Café design principles](https://theworldcafe.com/key-concepts-resources/design-principles/).

Liberating Structures' Wicked Questions makes tensions such as shared direction
and local autonomy explicit. This is useful for generating answerable conflicts
inside the piece. Its emphasis on complementary priorities should not be used to
pretend that all conflicting material interests can be reconciled.
[Wicked Questions](https://www.liberatingstructures.com/wicked-questions).

Jo Freeman argues that informal groups can develop elites whose power is hard to
see or hold accountable. This sharpens the question about how our own organising
rules distribute power, including the authority given to a composer that speaks
in the group's name.
[The Tyranny of Structurelessness](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm).
